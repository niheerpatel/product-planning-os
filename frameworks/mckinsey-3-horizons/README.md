# McKinsey Three Horizons Model — Framework Reference

**Authors**: Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley, David White
**Source**: *The Alchemy of Growth* (1999), McKinsey & Company
**Core idea**: Sustained growth requires a company to simultaneously manage three categories of business initiative — each operating on a different time horizon, with different risk profiles, management disciplines, and resource requirements. The model is a portfolio framework, not a sequential roadmap.

---

## The Three Horizons

### Horizon 1 — Extend and Defend the Core
The current core businesses that generate most of today's revenue and profit. The strategic task is to protect and incrementally extend this engine.

- **Time focus**: Now — 0 to 12 months
- **Objective**: Maximize and defend current cash flows
- **Activity type**: Operational improvement, incremental feature development, customer retention, efficiency gains
- **Risk level**: Low (well-understood markets, known customers)
- **Management style**: Execution-oriented, metrics-driven, process-optimized
- **Resource claim**: Typically receives the largest share of budget (~70% in the classic McKinsey allocation)

**Key question**: Are we extracting full value from what we already have, while defending against competitive erosion?

### Horizon 2 — Build Emerging Businesses
Rapidly growing businesses that are beginning to generate meaningful revenue, but not yet at scale. These are the mid-term bets that will become tomorrow's H1 businesses.

- **Time focus**: Near-term — 2 to 5 years
- **Objective**: Scale and establish new revenue streams that will replace H1 as it matures
- **Activity type**: Market expansion, new product lines, adjacency moves, partnerships and acquisitions
- **Risk level**: Medium (some market validation, but execution risk is high)
- **Management style**: Entrepreneurial within a structure; requires different metrics than H1
- **Resource claim**: Typically ~20% of budget

**Key question**: Have we identified which bets have proven enough to deserve scale-up investment, and are we actually scaling them?

### Horizon 3 — Create Viable Options
Early-stage seeds and options that could become significant businesses in the future. Many will fail; the goal is to have enough in the portfolio that some survive to H2.

- **Time focus**: Long-term — 5 to 12 years (original framework assumption)
- **Objective**: Explore, experiment, and create optionality for future growth
- **Activity type**: R&D, pilot programs, skunkworks, incubation, venture investments, capability acquisition
- **Risk level**: High (unproven markets, uncertain technology, early customer discovery)
- **Management style**: Highly exploratory; standard ROI metrics do not apply
- **Resource claim**: Typically ~10% of budget

**Key question**: Do we have a sufficiently diverse and adequately funded set of bets that one or two will matter in ten years?

---

## The Portfolio Allocation Logic

The 70-20-10 split (H1/H2/H3) is the most widely cited allocation heuristic, though Baghai, Coley, and White never mandated a universal ratio — the right split depends on industry clock speed, competitive pressure, and a company's growth ambitions. What the model insists on is that **all three horizons must be funded simultaneously**. Companies that starve H3 to protect H1 wake up one day with no replacement business. Companies that over-invest in H3 while neglecting H1 lose the cash engine needed to fund long-term exploration.

The framework explicitly rejects the idea that a company should "complete" H1 before investing in H2 or H3. The three horizons are concurrent, not sequential.

## Key Components and Principles

### 1. Different metrics for different horizons
Each horizon requires different success criteria:

| Metric | H1 | H2 | H3 |
|--------|----|----|-----|
| Primary | Revenue, margin, NPS | Revenue growth rate, customer acquisition | Number of experiments, option value, learning velocity |
| Secondary | Cost efficiency, churn | Time to profitability | Assumptions tested, pivots made |
| Failure signal | Margin erosion, market share loss | Slow adoption, failure to scale | No pipeline to H2 |

### 2. Different management disciplines
The same management team cannot optimally run all three horizons. H1 needs operators; H2 needs builders; H3 needs explorers. Companies that apply H1 management discipline to H3 initiatives systematically kill them (see: corporate innovation theater).

### 3. The migration path
The natural life cycle: ideas germinate in H3, get validated and scaled in H2, then generate cash as H1. As H1 matures, it faces commoditization and declining margins — requiring new H2 businesses to take over. This cycle is continuous.

```
H3 (explore) → H2 (scale) → H1 (harvest) → commoditization → repeat
```

### 4. Portfolio balance is dynamic
The right balance shifts over time. A startup is all-H3. A mature conglomerate may be largely H1. The question is whether the mix matches the company's strategic context and competitive environment.

## How to Apply the Model

### Step 1: Inventory and classify
Map every significant initiative, product line, R&D project, and investment to one of the three horizons. Be honest — most companies discover they are 90%+ H1.

### Step 2: Assess horizon health
- **H1 health check**: Is the core business growing, stable, or declining? How much runway does it have before commoditization?
- **H2 health check**: Are any H2 bets achieving proof-of-concept scale? Is there a clear path for any of them to become H1?
- **H3 health check**: Is there a real pipeline, or a fig-leaf "innovation lab" that receives token funding?

### Step 3: Identify gaps
Common patterns:
- No H3 pipeline → future growth starvation
- No H2 scaling → H3 ideas that never mature
- H2 ideas that get cannibalized or starved by H1's quarterly demands

### Step 4: Restructure allocation and governance
Separate the management systems for each horizon. H3 should not compete for resources on the same quarterly planning cycle as H1. Consider dedicated funding pools, separate P&Ls, or structural separation (e.g., innovation units, corporate venture arms).

### Step 5: Define "graduation" criteria
Articulate what success in H3 looks like before it can be moved to H2, and what H2 scale milestones must be hit before moving to H1. Without these gates, horizons blur and initiatives drift in limbo.

## Common Pitfalls

### 1. Treating the horizons as phases, not concurrent portfolios
The most common misreading. Companies "finish H1" before starting H2, which means they begin building the next business only after the current one is already in decline. The model requires all three to run in parallel at all times.

### 2. Applying H1 management and metrics to H3
H3 initiatives that must show quarterly ROI, get reviewed against H1 targets, or compete for headcount in annual planning cycles will always lose. This is how large companies systematically destroy early-stage innovation.

### 3. Using the model as a labeling exercise without resource reallocation
Naming things "H2" and "H3" without actually changing budget allocation or management structure produces no change. The model only works if the labels are connected to real resource decisions and governance differences.

### 4. Underfunding H2 relative to H3
Some companies invest in early exploration (H3) and run lots of pilots, but never provide the sustained capital required to scale a winner. H2 is the "valley of death" where many corporate innovations die — not because the idea was wrong, but because the company wouldn't commit to scaling it.

### 5. Horizon drift
H2 initiatives that fail to grow get reclassified as H3 (exploratory), or H3 experiments that look promising get forced into H1 timelines before they are ready. Clear criteria for graduation and demotion prevent this.

### 6. Ignoring adjacency traps in H2
H2 bets that look like logical adjacencies from H1 often carry hidden complexity — new customer segments, different regulatory environments, unfamiliar channels. The proximity to the core business can cause underestimation of what's actually required.

## Steve Blank's 2019 Critique — The Agile Disruption of the Time Dimension

In a widely read 2019 *Harvard Business Review* article, Steve Blank (Stanford professor, serial entrepreneur, father of the Lean Startup methodology) argued that the Three Horizons model's time assumptions had been broken by the speed of modern software and platform disruption.

**The original assumption**: H3 initiatives take 5–12 years to become material. This made sense in 1999, when building a new business required significant capital expenditure, physical infrastructure, and long sales cycles.

**Blank's argument**: In the modern era, a small startup with cloud infrastructure, agile development, and open-source tooling can go from H3 concept to H1-threatening competitor in 12–18 months — not 5–12 years. The time compression is not incremental; it is an order-of-magnitude change. Uber disrupted taxis in roughly 36 months. Netflix streaming disrupted DVD rental almost before Blockbuster recognized the threat. WeWork reached scale in years, not decades.

**The implication**: If disruption now happens at H3 speed on an H1 timeline, large companies that use the Three Horizons model's original time assumptions as justification for slow exploration are operationally blind. They may believe an H3 threat is "5–10 years away" when it is, in fact, already scaling.

**Blank's prescription**: Companies need to compress H3 exploration cycles using the same lean/agile/customer-discovery methods that startups use. The model is still valid as a portfolio concept, but the time dimensions must be recalibrated for industry clock speed. Some industries (software, fintech, consumer apps) have H3 cycles of 12–24 months. Capital-intensive industries (automotive, aerospace, energy) still have longer cycles, but should not assume that the 1999 timelines apply universally.

**Important nuance**: Blank did not argue the framework is obsolete — he argued it needs a software-era update. The portfolio logic (all three concurrent, different management, different metrics) remains sound.

## Real-World Examples

### Example 1: Amazon
Amazon is perhaps the most studied Three Horizons practitioner, though the model was never officially invoked.

- **H1 (core retail)**: E-commerce marketplace and fulfillment. Amazon has continuously invested in making H1 more efficient — Prime, same-day delivery, checkout optimization — while generating the cash that funds everything else.
- **H2 (scaling)**: AWS began as an internal infrastructure project in the mid-2000s and was methodically scaled into a commercial cloud platform through the late 2000s and early 2010s. Amazon Advertising followed a similar arc — built to serve internal needs, scaled into a separate business. Third-party seller services (Marketplace) is another example.
- **H3 (exploration)**: Alexa/Echo, Amazon Go (cashierless retail), Amazon Fresh, Kuiper (satellite internet), Amazon Pharmacy, and various healthcare ventures have occupied H3 at various points. Several failed quietly (Fire Phone); some graduated to H2; AWS itself was once in H3.

The Amazon case illustrates how H3 exploration at sufficient scale and patience produces breakthrough H2 and H1 businesses over a decade-long timeframe.

### Example 2: Apple (2001–2015)
Apple's transformation from a computer company into a consumer electronics and platform company illustrates H2 and H3 discipline at the CEO level.

- **H1**: Mac computers — defended aggressively with hardware and OS improvements, maintained as the cash and brand foundation.
- **H2**: iPod (2001) was an adjacent bet (consumer electronics + digital media distribution via iTunes) that scaled to become a dominant revenue contributor by 2004–2005. iPhone (2007) was an even larger H2 bet — it required massive investment and was genuinely uncertain, but Apple committed at scale.
- **H3**: Apple Watch, Apple TV, Apple Silicon (transition from Intel), and services (App Store, iCloud, Apple Music) all started as H3 bets. The App Store in particular was uncertain at launch and is now the highest-margin business Apple operates.

Note that Apple's H3 cycle behaved closer to Blank's compressed timeline — iPhone went from H3 concept to H1 dominance in under five years.

### Example 3: Ford Motor Company (relevant to context)
Ford's strategic challenges through the 2010s and into the 2020s illustrate both the difficulty of executing all three horizons and the cost of failing to do so.

- **H1 (core)**: Trucks (F-Series), SUVs, and commercial vehicles. The F-150 franchise has been Ford's most profitable H1 asset for decades. Ford's decision to exit passenger cars in North America (2018–2019) was an explicit H1 rationalization — doubling down on highest-margin core products.
- **H2 (scaling)**: Ford Pro (commercial fleet services, software, financing), Ford Blue (ICE optimization), and the F-150 Lightning electric truck represent H2 scaling bets. Ford Model e (EV division) was structured as a separate unit precisely to avoid H1 management discipline infecting an H2 scaling effort.
- **H3 (exploration)**: Ford's investment in Argo AI (autonomous vehicles), various mobility experiments under the "Ford Smart Mobility" umbrella in 2016–2019, and current investments in software-defined vehicle platforms occupy H3. The Argo AI shutdown (2022) was a costly H3 failure — illustrating the model's warning that H3 investments carry high failure rates and should be sized accordingly.

Ford's struggles also illustrate the Blank critique: Tesla moved from H3 startup to H1 competitor in the EV segment in roughly 8–10 years — far faster than Ford's planning cycles assumed was possible.

## Important Nuances and Limitations

**The model is descriptive, not prescriptive at the micro level**: It tells you what categories of initiative to maintain and why; it does not tell you which specific H3 bets to make or how to evaluate them. It must be combined with other frameworks (S-Curve analysis, Jobs-to-be-Done, competitive analysis) for specific investment decisions.

**Industry clock speed matters enormously**: The original 1999 time dimensions (H3 = 5–12 years) were calibrated to industrial-era businesses. Software, platform, and consumer businesses can operate on compressed timelines. Physical infrastructure, regulatory, and capital-intensive industries (energy, automotive, aerospace) still face longer cycles. Practitioners must recalibrate the time bands for their specific context.

**The 70-20-10 ratio is a starting heuristic, not a law**: A company in a rapidly declining H1 market may need to weight H2 far more heavily. A company with no H3 pipeline and a robust H1 may need to temporarily over-invest in H3. Context determines the right allocation.

**The model does not solve the organizational innovation problem**: Even companies that understand the Three Horizons intellectually often fail to execute because they cannot protect H3 and H2 initiatives from H1's quarterly demands. The model diagnoses the portfolio problem but does not prescribe the organizational design solution. That requires separate work on governance, incentives, and culture.

**Measurement ambiguity for H3**: The model acknowledges that standard financial metrics don't apply to H3, but is underspecified about what *should* be measured. This creates real governance challenges — H3 leaders often lack accountability, or face the wrong accountability. Innovation accounting (Eric Ries) and option-value frameworks attempt to fill this gap.
